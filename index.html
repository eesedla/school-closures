How closing a school changes a community

Old buildings, faded bricks and the fallout of a school closure

by Franziska Wild

The abandoned Robert Fulton Elementary school is the tallest building around for blocks. Stand anywhere in the neighborhood and its chimney remains visible on the horizon.

Neighbors say, when it was open, Fulton was a good school filled with good teachers. Today, the staircase inside the building has wholly collapsed. Its windows, where not covered in greying plywood, are broken. A sapling has taken root in one of its gutters.

Fulton isn’t unique.

This summer — against the backdrop of the decision to shutter the largest number of schools in Cleveland's history — Signal Cleveland set out to learn how school closures ripple into nearby neighborhoods. We analyzed census data, knocked on doors and went to block club meetings. What we found is that in most places the closures, like Fulton’s chimney, cast long shadows.

We narrowed in on a wave of 16 school closures from 2010 and 2011. In census tracts where schools closed during that time population fell by an average of 17% over the next decade. At the same time, the population in areas without closures declined by only 0.5% during the same period. And even reuse of school buildings didn’t necessarily stem these declines.

These numbers can’t tell the whole story of what these schools meant, but the people who stayed can. We had conversations with over 30 neighbors who live around these schools — and in many cases attended them. They told us these schools functioned as civic institutions and sites of memory and that their closures affected their quality of life.

Life across from an abandoned school

Kisha and Harris McMichael’s home sits face to face with Fulton.

Kisha attended Fulton and can't help but think fondly of how it used to be. She remembers its playground filled with kids and a report she wrote on the school's namesake, Robert Fulton, who is credited with inventing the steamboat. Now, the school’s hulking shell is a source of bitterness to the point that Kisha’s voice strains and catches a bit while discussing it.

“It's just disappointing,” she said. “You know, Robert Fulton was a good school.”

The couple lives in the home where Kisha grew up. But it’s almost been entirely rebuilt following a house fire in 2015. Listening to Harris, who works in construction, talk through the process of rebuilding the house himself — including drawing his own blueprints — it’s clear what he’s poured into the home. That only adds to his frustration when it comes to Fulton, which Harris sees as a hurdle to selling or renting the couple’s house some day.

“Look over there,” Harris said, gesturing in the direction of the school. “It depreciates the value of our home, of everything.”

Fulton closed in 2010 after serving the community for more than 80 years. In 2021, the city awarded the building to a suburban development firm for use as housing, but that deal fell apart, district records show. For now, the building remains on the school district’s books, with no plans to tear it down anytime soon.

Down the block, Erick Walker, who like Kisha went to Fulton, has been trying to get answers about the school for a long time. The neat blue home he lives in now has been in his family since he moved there with his mother and sister as a 12-year-old. He’s now in his late fifties.

He’s spent the last six years advocating for answers about the school. That means attending city council meetings, writing to the mayor and being interviewed by the TV news. He even launched a city council campaign in 2024 with the hope it might nudge some action on the school.

Watching Fulton deteriorate bothers Walker. He knows it’s a drag on the neighborhood’s property values. But it also goes deeper than that, for him the school is also a symbol of how his community has been neglected.

“It’s frustration that nobody seems to do anything about it,” he said. “It’s anger that I’m being ignored. That whomever the powers may be doesn’t value our community enough to do anything about it.”

Over the years, since Fulton closed, Walker and the McMichael’s have watched the blocks around the school change. From 2010 to 2024 the census tract containing the school — an Oklahoma shaped area bordered by Milverton Rd. and Kinsman Ave on the north and south sides and by East 140th and East 149th on the west and east sides — lost a third of its residents.

They believe that closure of the school had “absolutely” something to do with that decline. At one point this winter, Walker called a senior neighbor of his to check up on her only to learn she’d moved away, a decision she blamed on crime and Fulton’s closure.

Catherine Wright is that neighbor. She remembers the school changing the neighborhood greatly, in part because when Fulton closed, “the joy and laughter of children coming up and down the street” disappeared.

“The police car was there, so the kids wouldn't get hurt,” she told Signal Cleveland. “We had a school crossing guard that was at the end of the street…kids were able to go to the store. It was actually really, really safe.”

Then there’s all the negative attention the empty school has attracted since it closed. People will routinely break into Fulton and dump trash including mattresses and pieces of furniture on the school grounds, Harris said. White also recalls watching birds of prey flying over the building in long, lazy circles presumably attracted by something, like a dead animal, decaying inside.

Since the city began publicly logging 311 calls in summer of 2017 there have been over xx calls for illegal dumping in a radius just a quarter mile out from Fulton. The feeling of deteriorating safety has gotten so bad to the point where Kisha no longer visits the corner store she used to love.

At this point, Fulton’s neighbors just want the building torn down. They’re sick and tired of reporting break-ins, illegal dumping and people setting off fireworks on the Fourth of July. They’re also worried that at some point they’ll have to call about something more serious than a mound of old tires or someone coming to steal the wrought iron fence piece by piece.

“They need to tear it down,” Kisha said. “It just makes the neighborhood look horrible.”

Fulton isn’t unique

The district shuttered 18 school buildings this year. District officials were adamant the cause was fewer kids — a result of population and birthrate declines. They made the case that without closures CMSD can’t offer students the educational opportunities they deserve.

In response, community leaders warned that closing schools would only push more families from their neighborhoods. Current forecasts predict, a decade from now, the district will have lost an eighth of its current students.

Greg Wheeler, an alumnus of the now-closed Collinwood High School who grew up in the Glenville neighborhood, has spent a lot of time thinking about the chicken-and-egg nature of this problem. For more than a decade, he’s coached football at Collinwood High School, work he describes as being about “saving and transforming lives.”

At the same time, he’s watched as families have left the neighborhood for nearby suburbs. He believes that, in the past, a continuum of neighborhood schools helped keep families rooted from kindergarten through high school. Wheeler blames a mix of school closures and the district’s embrace of open enrollment and speciality schools through the Cleveland Plan for disrupting neighborhood schools and concentrating educational opportunities on the West Side of the city.

Now, “our neighborhoods suffer because our best and brightest often flee for a better life,” he reflected in a long email to me on the eve of Collinwood’s last day this spring. But he’s also realistic that this isn’t just about schools.

“Everybody has to be held accountable, but our focus is just on school,” he said, reflecting on the lack of grocery stores, community spaces and other amenities beyond schools. “I think that there needs to be some equality across neighborhoods. There are certain neighborhoods that get more or seem like they get more than others.”

Council Member Mike Polensek, who represents Collinwood and is a graduate of its historic high school, has loudly laid the blame at the feet of the district and the mayor who appoints its board.

“The various neighborhoods who’ve had the highest population losses,” he said. “This is where they have decimated the public school system.… Unless we figure out how to stabilize the school system, we cannot stabilize the city.”

Driving with Wheeler down St. Clair Avenue from where the high school sits at the five points intersection to where he grew up just off of East 152nd Street, the impact of school closures is clear. Every few blocks he could turn into a side street and find a residential pocket where a school once stood — or now stands empty.

In the Northeast quadrant of the city, home to both Collinwood and Glenville, we found records of nearly 20 schools that have closed since 2000.
